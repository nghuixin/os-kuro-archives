# Supplement review: what Kuro took, and how strong the evidence is

Between 2022 and his death in Oct 2026 Kuro was given more than 40 supplement products ([`prescriptions.csv`](../../data/processed/clinical/prescriptions.csv), [`invoice_items.csv`](../../data/processed/clinical/invoice_items.csv), the [DAP diet answers](../../data/dog_aging_project/dap_surveys_kuro.csv)). Brand-specific trials rarely exist, so this review groups the products by **active ingredient** and asks: for the reason Kuro was given it, how good is the evidence in **dogs**?

This is a desk review of published evidence, not veterinary advice. It judges the evidence, not the decisions made at the time.

## How the evidence is graded

Each ingredient is graded for **the reason Kuro was given it**. If he had it for two reasons, it gets a grade for each. **Evidence in dogs takes priority:** when dog trials exist, the grade comes from them, even if results in humans point the other way.

| Grade | What it means | What it takes to earn this grade |
|---|---|---|
| **Clinically validated** | It works in dogs, for the reason Kuro had it. | At least two randomised controlled trials (RCTs) with consistent results, or a meta-analysis that pools RCTs and finds a benefit. The trials are in **client-owned dogs** (pets with the disease that developed naturally, not lab-induced). They measure **how the dog does**, such as pain, mobility or survival, not just lab values. |
| **Validated, but for another use** | It works in dogs, but for a condition Kuro wasn't shown to have. | Meets the bar above, or is the established standard treatment, for a **different** condition. Examples: digestive enzymes for exocrine pancreatic insufficiency, B12 for proven deficiency. This grade says nothing about whether it helped Kuro. |
| **Established nutrition practice** | It's standard care because of nutrient requirements, not because of trials. | It's needed to meet published nutrient requirements, for example to balance a home-cooked diet. What matters is whether the dose fits the diet. |
| **Limited clinical evidence** | It may help dogs, but the trials aren't strong enough to be sure. | There are trials in dogs with the condition, but they have at least one major weakness. They're small, or there's only one; they were funded or run by the manufacturer; the results are mixed; the ingredient was tested only as part of a combination product; or they measured lab values (such as liver enzymes) rather than how the dog does. |
| **Proven in humans, not yet in dogs** | It works in people, but nobody has properly tested it in dogs. | RCTs or a meta-analysis in **humans** show a clinical benefit for the equivalent condition. But there are **no clinical trials in dogs** with that condition; at most there are lab, safety or healthy-dog studies. Dogs differ from people in metabolism, dosing and disease, so success in humans is a reason to test in dogs, not proof that it works. |
| **Research stage** | It's plausible, but it hasn't been proven for this use in any species. | The only evidence is from cell or lab studies, rodent models, experiments in laboratory dogs, small or early human studies, biomarker changes in healthy dogs, or uncontrolled pilots with no comparison group. |
| **Tested and not supported** | It was tested in dogs for this purpose and didn't work. | At least one well-designed controlled trial, or a meta-analysis, in dogs found no meaningful benefit, or found harm. This grade outranks positive results from pilots or human studies. |
| **No credible evidence** | There's no good reason to expect it to work. | No clinical studies were found, or the only support is the manufacturer's unpublished claims, or there's no plausible mechanism (for example, homeopathic remedies diluted until no molecules of the original substance remain). |

**In short:**
- **Only one supplement group was clinically validated for its purpose:** omega-3s for joint pain. Kuro had a dedicated fish-oil product only in 2022, though his 2024 raw diet supplied a meaningful dose.
- **Two groups were tested in dogs and failed:** glucosamine/chondroitin for arthritis pain, and turkey tail for tumour survival.
- **B12 and digestive enzymes are proven only for specific deficiencies,** and Kuro was never tested for either.
- **Most of the rest is research-stage or has no credible evidence.**

## Notes by group

### Joints

- **Omega-3 (EPA + DHA): clinically validated.** Three randomised trials in 2010 fed dogs with osteoarthritis diets rich in fish oil. They improved weight-bearing in a force-plate study of 38 dogs, owner-rated function in a 127-dog study across 18 clinics, and lameness and weight-bearing at the highest dose in a dose-titration study. A 2022 systematic review and meta-analysis of nutraceuticals for osteoarthritis pain concluded that omega-3s are the supplement with convincing evidence. The caveats: effects are modest, several outcomes were owner-rated, and the benefit depends on the dose of EPA + DHA, best delivered through a therapeutic diet or a measured fish-oil dose.
- **Green-lipped mussel: limited clinical evidence.** Several small randomised trials, mostly of the lipid extract PCSO-524 (Antinol), report better lameness and weight-bearing scores. But many were funded by the manufacturer and run by the same investigators, and a 2022 crossover trial of a mussel, curcumin and blackcurrant product found only partial benefit over placebo. A 2023 randomised trial in 75 dogs with hip arthritis (Kampa) found PCSO-524 improved weight-bearing about as much as carprofen, but it was small (14–16 dogs per group). It's reasonable to try, but not proven.
- **Glucosamine / chondroitin: tested and not supported.** The 2022 meta-analysis found no analgesic effect for canine osteoarthritis and recommended against prescribing them for pain.
- **Turmeric / curcumin: limited for joints.** The two randomised trials both tested combination products (curcumin with collagen and green tea; curcumin with mussel and blackcurrant), so curcumin's own effect isn't isolated. The results were modest or partial.
  - **In humans: stronger than in dogs.** Meta-analyses of randomised trials in knee osteoarthritis find that curcumin reduces pain compared with placebo, including when it's given on its own rather than in a blend. The benefit for function is less consistent, most trials are small and of low to moderate quality, and the formulations and doses were made for people. Because there are dog trials, the dog grade (limited) stands.

**For Kuro:** his joint supplements were the weaker options (glucosamine, mussel) rather than a measured omega-3 dose. His **prescribed analgesics** (Galliprant, gabapentin) and acupuncture carried most of the pain control in 2025–26.

### Liver

- **SAMe + silybin (Samylin): limited clinical evidence.**
  - The one randomised trial in dogs (Denamarin with the chemotherapy drug lomustine) found smaller rises in liver enzymes: 68% of dogs on the supplement versus 84% without it. That was prevention during chemotherapy, a different situation from Kuro's.
  - SAMe alone didn't prevent the liver changes caused by prednisolone in a controlled study. That's relevant, because Kuro was on prednisolone for long periods.
  - There are no randomised trials in dogs with established liver disease.
  - It's widely used, safe and plausible, but the benefit isn't proven.
- **Milk thistle and herbal liver tonics: research stage.** A 2013 review found no randomised trials of milk thistle in animals with liver disease; the dog data come from poisoning experiments. Blends like Adored Beast Liver Tonic have no product-level studies.
- **Curcumin for the liver: research stage.**

**For Kuro:** at times he had **three liver products at once** (Samylin, a liver tonic and Maximus Hep), and none has strong evidence. A single well-chosen product with a defined dose would have been easier to keep stocked; the archive records Samylin running out unnoticed.

### Gut and pancreas

- **Probiotics: limited clinical evidence.**
  - A 2019 systematic review of 17 trials found small, mostly biased studies.
  - In chronic gut disease, diet did the work and probiotics added no significant benefit.
  - A 2024 meta-analysis found nutraceuticals didn't shorten acute diarrhoea by a clinically meaningful amount.
  - Probiotics are low-risk and may help in stress- or antibiotic-related diarrhoea.
  - **In humans: proven for one specific use.** For children taking antibiotics, a 2019 Cochrane review of 33 trials found that high-dose probiotics (at least 5 billion CFU a day) prevent antibiotic-related diarrhoea; about 6 children need to be treated to prevent one case. That supports the antibiotic use above, but not chronic gut disease or pancreatitis. The benefit depends on the strain and dose, and human strains and doses don't transfer directly to dogs.
- **Digestive enzymes: validated, but for another use.** Enzyme replacement is the established treatment for **exocrine pancreatic insufficiency (EPI)**, where the pancreas stops producing enzymes. Kuro had **pancreatitis**, not diagnosed EPI. No enzyme test (TLI) is on record, and there's no good evidence that enzymes help pancreatitis or prevent flares.
- **Slippery elm and herbal gut blends: research stage / traditional use.** Activated charcoal is used for poisoning; there's no evidence for routine daily gut support.

### Tumour and immunity

- **Turkey tail (Coriolus versicolor PSP): tested and not supported.**
  - A 2012 pilot of 15 dogs with splenic haemangiosarcoma and no placebo group reported unusually long survival, and was widely publicised.
  - The **2022 randomised trial** (Gedney et al.) found that PSP **did not extend survival** compared with standard chemotherapy, and **females on PSP alone did worse**.
  - **In humans: positive, but for a different product and setting.** PSK (Krestin), a different extract from the same mushroom, is used in Japan alongside chemotherapy after surgery for gastric and colorectal cancer. A meta-analysis of eight randomised trials in gastric cancer (8,009 patients) and a 2017 network meta-analysis of 23 trials in gut cancers both found better survival when PSK was added to chemotherapy. These were mostly Japanese and Chinese trials of a pharmaceutical-grade extract given **with chemotherapy**, not a mushroom supplement on its own. They don't override the dog trial.
- **Other mushroom blends** (CAS Options, Vital Defense): no dog trials.

**For Kuro:** his tumour was never typed, so it isn't known whether it was a haemangiosarcoma. Even so, the best trial didn't support turkey tail for survival.

### Vitamins, minerals and nerves

- **Vitamin B12: validated, but for another use.** B12 reliably corrects **proven deficiency**. A 2018 randomised trial in dogs with chronic gut disease found oral tablets worked as well as injections. **There's no B12 test in Kuro's records**, so it's unknown whether he was deficient. There's no evidence B12 relieves nerve pain. Neuroforte's ingredients aren't on record.
- **Multivitamin / mineral: established nutrition practice.** A home-cooked diet needs a balancing supplement to meet nutrient requirements; that's standard veterinary nutrition rather than a trial question. The point is to **balance** the diet, not to add more: his 2024 recipe was already near the vitamin D limit and high in iron (see the [diet review](./diet-review.md)), so an extra multivitamin could push some nutrients over.

### Kidney

- **Aminavast: no credible evidence.** The manufacturer cites unpublished trials. No independent, peer-reviewed study of this product was found; the published trials of kidney supplements in dogs test different products. **Diet and monitoring** are the established care for kidney disease.

### Longevity

- **Resveratrol: research stage.** There are safety studies, biomarker changes in healthy dogs, and mixed cognition results. No study shows longer or healthier life in dogs.

### Homeopathy and cell salts

- **Homeopathy (Arnica, Hypericum, Strophanthus): no credible evidence.** Systematic reviews of veterinary homeopathy trials found few reliable trials and very limited evidence of any effect beyond placebo. At 30C the remedy is diluted so far that no molecules of the original substance remain. The remedies themselves are harmless; the risk is **delaying effective pain relief**.
- **Cell salts: no credible evidence.** No clinical studies in dogs were found.

### Chinese herbal formulas

- **Xiao Chai Hu Tang: research stage, with safety signals.**
  - In humans it's linked to **interstitial pneumonitis** and **liver injury**.
  - Guidance advises against it in liver disease and with **low platelets**. Kuro had a bleeding-prone tumour and was anaemic.
  - The evidence for benefit is mostly from animal models.
- **Si Miao San: research stage.** The evidence is mouse studies of gout and a human review protocol. No dog trials were found.
- **Product quality:** herbal products vary in content and can be contaminated, and none of these formulas has been trialled in dogs.

## Safety points relevant to Kuro

- **Bleeding risk (theoretical, unstudied):**
  - Curcumin, fish oil and green-lipped mussel all have mild anti-clotting effects in other contexts.
  - Kuro had a tumour that bled into his abdomen in 2024 and had recurrent anaemia.
  - No study tests these supplements in dogs with bleeding tumours, so this is a caution, not evidence of harm.
  - It's the kind of combination worth listing for the vet.
- **Many products at once:** the July 2024 and June 2025 care lists each have about seven supplements given at the same time, alongside prescription medicines (prednisolone, and later Galliprant and gabapentin).
  - No single list was kept. The archive records supplements running out unnoticed, and products given without anyone reviewing the combination.
  - Fewer products, each chosen for evidence, would have been simpler to give, stock and review.

## Why supplements are given without strong evidence

Most of Kuro's products had limited or no evidence, yet they're routinely recommended. When trials are missing, day-to-day practice rests on the following.

| What practice rests on | What the sources show | Kuro's examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Mechanism, and borrowing from human medicine** | The ingredient does something in the lab or in people that ought to help, so it's assumed to help dogs. The 2019 ACVIM consensus on chronic hepatitis in dogs drew on specialists' opinion and the veterinary literature, plus some human liver literature. A plausible mechanism doesn't guarantee benefit: milk thistle protects the liver in lab experiments, but failed in a large human trial (see the next table). | SAMe + silybin, milk thistle, curcumin, resveratrol |
| **Expert consensus** | Where trials are missing, specialist bodies issue consensus statements from expert agreement. This is the lowest rung of the formal evidence hierarchy, but it's what many clinicians follow. | Liver products |
| **Prevention and what owners ask for** | In a survey of 126 vets (Elrod & Hofmeister 2019), they mostly recommended nutraceuticals **for prevention or because clients were interested**, and named safety and efficacy as their main considerations. A specialist review notes there are no definitive studies of why owners use supplements; clinical experience points to doubts about commercial food and a preference for "natural" products. | Multivitamins, "healthy ageing" products, probiotics |
| **Clinical impression** | Owners and vets see a dog improve and credit the supplement. In a controlled arthritis trial (Conzemius & Evans 2012), owners judged dogs **on placebo** improved about 40% of the time, and vets about 43–45%, while force-plate measurements showed no change. The effect grew over time. | Joint supplements, homeopathy |
| **The rules and the market** | In the US there's no "dietary supplement" category for animal products; they're regulated as foods or drugs, and products sold without drug claims don't have to prove they work. The industry's quality body (NASC) doesn't require efficacy studies. Rules differ by country. | Aminavast, YuMove, mushroom blends |
| **Traditional Chinese veterinary medicine** | Diagnosis is by "pattern" (for example, "Spleen Qi deficiency") rather than disease, and herbs are given as multi-ingredient formulas matched to the pattern. The framework is taught through practitioner texts and courses (Xie & Preast). A 2024 overview of veterinary reviews found 171 herbal-medicine trials across species, but every review of them was low quality, so the trials couldn't be reliably assessed. | Xiao Chai Hu Tang, Si Miao San |
| **Homeopathy** | Based on "like cures like" and on extreme dilution. The UK veterinary regulator (RCVS, 2017) stated there's no recognised body of evidence for homeopathy and that it isn't based on sound scientific principles. Treatments like this must not delay or replace evidence-based care. | Arnica, Hypericum, Strophanthus |
| **Cell salts** | Devised by the German doctor Wilhelm Schüßler in 1873, on the idea that disease comes from mineral imbalances in cells. Schüßler rejected homeopathy's "like cures like" but used similar dilutions. No trials or systematic reviews of their effect were found. | Ferrum phos and others |

## Tested and found harmless, but without benefit

Some supplements have been tested properly and found to do **no harm, but no good**. This is useful evidence: it shows the product isn't worth the cost and the extra pills, even though it's safe.

| Supplement | What was tested | Benefit | Harm |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Glucosamine / chondroitin** | Randomised trial of 75 pet dogs with hip arthritis (Kampa 2023): glucosamine/chondroitin vs green-lipped mussel extract (PCSO-524), another marine oil, carprofen and placebo | **None significant.** Weight-bearing improved with the mussel extract, the marine oil and carprofen, but not with glucosamine/chondroitin | Not reported as a concern in the sources checked |
| **Probiotic on top of diet** | Placebo-controlled trial of 21 dogs with diet-responsive diarrhoea on an elimination diet (Sauter 2006) | **None added.** All dogs improved, on probiotic or placebo | None reported in the sources checked |
| **SAMe during steroid treatment** | Controlled study of dogs given prednisolone (Center 2005) | **Didn't prevent** the liver changes caused by prednisolone | None reported in the sources checked |
| **Milk thistle (silymarin), in humans** | Randomised trial of 154 people with chronic hepatitis C, at 3–5 times the usual dose for 24 weeks (Fried 2012) | **None.** Liver enzymes, viral load and quality of life were no better than placebo | Side effects were similar to placebo |
| **Homeopathy** | Randomised trial of 75 dogs with fear of fireworks (Cracknell & Mills 2008) | **None over placebo.** Owners reported improvement in both groups | The remedy itself is inert; the risk is delaying effective treatment |

Turkey tail isn't in this table: it showed no survival benefit, but females given it alone did worse, so it can't be called harmless.

## What the evidence would have supported

| Need | Best-supported option | Grade |
|---|---|---|
| Arthritis pain | Prescribed analgesics, plus omega-3 at a measured EPA + DHA dose (or a therapeutic joint diet) | Validated |
| Second-line for joints | Green-lipped mussel extract | Limited |
| Liver enzymes | SAMe + silybin, after ruling out causes (including prednisolone) | Limited |
| Gut upsets | Diet first; a probiotic is low-risk | Limited |
| Home-cooked diet | One balancing supplement, matched to the recipe | Established practice |
| Suspected B12 or enzyme deficiency | **Test first** (serum cobalamin, TLI), then supplement if low | Validated for confirmed deficiency |
